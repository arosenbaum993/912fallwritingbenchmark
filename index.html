Source #1

Read the policy summary about purposeful technology use.

More Than Access: Closing the Digital Use Divide

Adapted classroom-practice text based on the 2024 National Educational Technology Plan

1For years, conversations about educational technology focused on access: Did students have a device? Could they connect to the internet? Those questions still matter, but the 2024 National Educational Technology Plan argues that access alone does not guarantee better learning. A student can spend an entire day on a laptop and still use technology only to complete digital worksheets or watch information chosen by someone else.

2The plan calls this problem the digital use divide. On one side are students who use technology to investigate questions, build products, analyze information, collaborate, and receive useful feedback. On the other side are students whose screen time mostly replaces paper tasks without changing the thinking required. Both groups may have devices, but they do not have the same learning opportunities.

3Purposeful use can expand what a classroom makes possible. A science class might gather real-time environmental data and compare it with information from another region. A history class might examine digitized primary sources that would otherwise be unavailable. A student who needs text-to-speech, captions, translation, enlarged text, or an alternative way to demonstrate learning may gain access that print alone cannot provide. In these situations, technology is not merely present; it removes a barrier or allows students to do work that would be difficult without it.

4However, the plan also identifies a digital design divide: teachers and school leaders do not always receive enough time, training, or support to design strong technology-enabled lessons. When schools purchase devices without investing in instructional planning, technology can become the automatic format rather than a deliberate choice. The result may be more screen time without deeper learning.

5Closing the divide does not require using devices in every lesson. It requires asking whether a tool helps students create, explore, communicate, or think more deeply. Sometimes the best design may be a simulation or collaborative document; at other times, it may be a printed novel, a face-to-face discussion, or handwritten problem solving. The central issue is the quality of the learning task, not the number of glowing screens in the room.

Source note: This passage is an original instructional adaptation of the U.S. Department of Education, Office of Educational Technology, 2024 National Educational Technology Plan: A Call to Action for Closing the Digital Access, Design, and Use Divides.

Source #2

Read the data report about devices and distraction.

When the Screen Competes With the Lesson

Adapted classroom-practice text based on OECD PISA 2022 findings

1Digital devices can support learning, but they can also compete for a student's attention. The Programme for International Student Assessment, known as PISA, surveyed 15-year-old students in countries around the world in 2022. On average across participating OECD countries, about one in three students reported becoming distracted by using digital devices in most or every mathematics lesson. About one in four reported being distracted by classmates who were using devices.

2The pattern was connected to achievement. Students who said classmates' device use distracted them in at least some mathematics lessons scored 15 points lower, on average, than students who said it almost never happened, after researchers accounted for students' and schools' socioeconomic backgrounds. This finding shows an association, not proof that devices alone caused the score difference. Still, the consistent pattern across many education systems makes distraction difficult to dismiss.

3The same research warns against a simple conclusion that all classroom technology is harmful. Students who used digital devices for learning at school for a moderate amount of time generally performed better in mathematics than students who reported no use. The results changed when devices were used for leisure. Students reporting more leisure use at school tended to perform less well, especially as that time increased.

4How devices are used also matters. Students appeared less likely to report distraction when notifications were turned off, when they did not feel pressure to answer messages, and when device use was connected to teacher-led instruction. By contrast, frequent smartphone use created more opportunities for social messages, games, and unrelated browsing to interrupt concentration.

5Some schools respond with phone bans or general device rules. PISA findings suggest that a rule is only as strong as its design and enforcement. Many students in schools with phone bans still reported using a phone daily. A broad rule posted in a handbook may have little effect if expectations are unclear or inconsistently applied.

6The findings point toward a balanced policy: protect periods of sustained attention, reduce nonacademic device use, and keep digital tools when they clearly strengthen the lesson. The question is not simply whether a classroom contains technology. It is whether the technology supports the task or competes with it.

Source note: This passage is an original instructional adaptation of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, PISA 2022 Results, Volume II, sections on learning time, disciplinary climate, and digital distraction.

Source #3

Read the international report summary about conditions for effective technology.

Technology Should Serve Learners

Adapted classroom-practice text based on UNESCO's 2023 Global Education Monitoring Report

1Technology is often advertised as a solution to problems in education, but a tool can create new problems when schools adopt it without clear evidence or safeguards. UNESCO's 2023 Global Education Monitoring Report examined technology in education across many countries. Its central recommendation is that decisions should begin with learners' needs, not with the newest product.

2The report recognizes important benefits. Technology can connect students in remote areas to instruction, provide accessible formats for students with disabilities, and maintain learning during emergencies. Digital resources can also offer teachers new ways to present information, assess understanding, and communicate with families.

3Yet access is uneven. A lesson that assumes every student has reliable internet, a quiet place to work, or an updated device may deepen existing inequalities. Even when a school supplies devices, students may receive very different learning experiences depending on whether teachers have preparation, technical support, and time to choose effective materials.

4Privacy and commercial influence create additional concerns. Many education platforms collect information about students. Schools may not always know what is gathered, how long it is stored, or whether it is used to shape advertising and product development. A platform's popularity does not prove that it improves learning, and a company's claim should not replace independent evidence.

5UNESCO therefore identifies three conditions for effective educational technology: equitable access, appropriate rules and protections, and sufficient teacher preparation. It also argues that technology should support, rather than replace, the human relationships at the center of education. Discussion, feedback, collaboration, and trust cannot be measured only by minutes logged into a program.

6A useful technology policy must be selective. Schools should identify the learning problem first, compare digital and nondigital options, examine evidence, protect student information, and review whether the tool actually works. This process may lead to more technology in one lesson and less in another. What matters is whether the choice serves the student.

Source note: This passage is an original instructional adaptation of UNESCO, Global Education Monitoring Report 2023: Technology in Education - A Tool on Whose Terms?

Literature & Composition II Writer's Checklist

In my well-written text, I made sure to:

  • provide an introduction that guides the focus and captures the audience.
  • clearly establish my claim(s) and/or position on the topic.
  • develop a cohesive and effective organizational structure appropriate for the task.
  • use a consistently appropriate style and tone.
  • provide reasons that support my claim(s) and/or position.
  • support my reasons with ideas and evidence from multiple sources.
  • credit sources when integrating quotations or paraphrased ideas.
  • elaborate using multiple techniques and rhetorical devices or appeals.
  • use varied transitions to connect reasons, claims, and ideas.
  • provide a strong conclusion.
  • use varied syntax, correct verb tense, aspect, mood, and parallel structure.
  • correct errors in spelling, capitalization, punctuation, usage, and grammar.
